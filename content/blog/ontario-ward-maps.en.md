Ontario voted for its municipal councils on October 26, 2026, and the councils elected that day take office on November 15, 2026. Your ward decides which councillor represents you, which ballot you received and which ward office answers your questions. Many Ontario municipalities publish their ward boundaries as open data, and CanQuery draws each of these layers as an interactive map. This guide lists the ward maps of 20 Ontario municipalities, what each layer records and how to find your ward on it.

|---|---|

| New council term | Four years from November 15, 2026 |
| Municipalities in this guide | 20, from Toronto (25 wards) to Pickering (3 wards) |
| What each layer holds | One polygon per ward with its number, often its name, and in some cities a councillor |
| On CanQuery | An interactive map of each layer, with the publisher's original download |

## Find your ward on a map

1. Pick your municipality in the table below and open its map.
2. Zoom to your street; the basemap shows street names. Each coloured polygon is one ward.
3. Select the polygon that covers your address. Its fields give the ward number, and in many cities the ward's name or councillor.
4. Near a boundary, zoom in further: a ward line often follows the middle of a street, a rail line or a creek.

Council pages and municipal notices usually refer to the ward number, so note both the number and the name. If you cannot tell which side of a line an address falls on, your municipality's clerk can confirm it.

## Read councillor names with care

A councillor name in a boundary layer is the name the municipality recorded when it last updated the layer. Several of these layers still name the council elected in 2022. The councils elected on October 26, 2026 take office on November 15, 2026, and each municipality updates its layer on its own schedule. Before you write to a councillor, check the municipality's council page; the layer is a good way to find your ward, not a directory of who holds the seat today.

Some layers name regional councillors as well: the Whitby and Pickering layers list regional councillors, who also sit on Durham Regional Council.

## Check which boundary set you are looking at

Several municipalities publish more than one ward layer, and a layer's title is usually the best guide to the term it describes:

- Ottawa publishes [Wards 2026-2030](/datasets/arcgis-925dfae1c59a45f6b1f643366ce74b37-277) and [Wards 2022-2026](/datasets/arcgis-8973061e1b0c4cd09b4495088c04c310-0), each with 24 wards, as well as historical sets.
- Windsor has a second layer, [municipal ward boundary](/datasets/arcgis-051e67e0246642c39ff2bfee66b7e58a-4), with the same 10 wards.
- Peel Region's [WardBoundary](/datasets/arcgis-513fd67039084e2e987ef209576f7ed6-0) layer combines the wards of Brampton, Mississauga and Caledon in 27 polygons.

A date shown on a CanQuery dataset page is when the publisher's catalogue entry last changed, not the date a boundary took effect. When a layer's title or description does not say which election it serves, check with the municipality before relying on it for a past or future election.

## Check the official source

Each CanQuery dataset page links the municipality's own open-data page, which carries the description, the licence and the original downloads. The dates of voting day and of the council term are set by Ontario's [Municipal Elections Act, 1996](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/96m32), sections 5 and 6. Questions about ward boundaries or representation belong with the municipality's clerk; CanQuery provides the maps and the links to the sources.

When you share a ward map, include the layer's title, its CanQuery dataset page and the date you consulted it.

Each layer is published under its municipality's open data licence, shown on its CanQuery dataset page.
